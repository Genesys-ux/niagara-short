The first suspension bridge across Niagara Gorge was started with a five-dollar kite.
In January eighteen forty-eight, engineer Charles Ellet promised to link America and Canada over eight hundred feet of boiling rapids.
There was just one problem: nobody could get the first wire across.
Boats would get shredded in seconds.
Cannons couldn't shoot a heavy line that far without snapping it.
Rival engineers laughed and said he'd have better luck training a flock of eagles to carry it.
So Ellet offered a five-dollar prize to any kid who could fly a kite from cliff to cliff.
Sixteen-year-old Homan Walsh hiked to the windy Canadian rim with a homemade kite named The Union.
On his first try, the freezing night wind dropped his line onto jagged river ice, snapping it clean in two.
But how could a thin cotton string hold up an iron bridge?
You see, you never haul the heavy stuff first.
When Homan repaired his kite and landed it across the chasm, workers tied a strong cord to his twine.
That cord pulled a thick rope, the rope hauled an iron cable, and soon carriages were rolling across the sky—all started by a five-dollar kite.
